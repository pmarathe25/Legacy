# Game Concept Overview

## Premise

Humans descend from an alien people whose homeworld orbited a dying sun. As their sun expanded and their world became less habitable, they sent explorer vessels into space to search for places where their people could survive.

One vessel crashed on Earth. Its crew became stranded and lost contact with their homeworld. Earth’s stronger gravity changed their descendants over many generations. The original aliens were taller and thinner than modern humans because they evolved under lower gravity. They may have looked somewhat like elves.

The alien species and its civilization still need names. The exact state of their homeworld and the period in which the game takes place also remain open.

## The reincarnating ruler

The alien civilization is led by one chosen ruler. When the ruler dies, they are later reincarnated as a new person.

Two immortal guardians protect this line of rulers. After each death, they search for the next incarnation, identify them, teach them, and help them take power. Once the new ruler is ready, the guardians serve as their right and left hands.

Each incarnation is a different person with their own childhood, personality, skills, relationships, and experiences. The ruler can draw on the memories and abilities of earlier lives.

In battle, the ruler may call on one or more past lives. Using one life grants access to that person’s skills. Combining several lives can create very powerful effects. This has a cost that still needs to be decided. Possible costs include physical strain, loss of identity, emotional instability, reduced lifespan, or temporary loss of control.

The guardians develop differently. They live continuously and change slowly over time. Their abilities are dependable and less costly, but they do not have the ruler’s sudden bursts of power.

Past lives should also affect government. Former rulers may provide knowledge of old laws, lost crafts, military tactics, diplomacy, or earlier political relationships. Their mistakes and unfinished work may return as problems for later incarnations.

## Game structure

The game combines two main modes.

### Realm management

The main interface is a real-time kingdom simulation. The player rules a realm made of provinces. Provinces contain settlements, and settlements contain named areas such as:

- the town center;
- nearby farms;
- an artisan district;
- forests;
- mines;
- docks;
- temples;
- military camps.

Areas have travel times between them, but the game does not simulate exact paths or character movement across a tile grid.

Settlements begin small, with dozens of individually simulated people. Every citizen has a name, personality, skills, needs, relationships, possessions, dreams, goals, and plans.

As the realm grows, the player can appoint mayors and governors. They handle local decisions according to their ability, personality, loyalty, and instructions. The player can still intervene directly when needed.

### Party missions

The player directly controls the ruler and gives firm orders to the two guardians. They can be sent on expeditions, into battles, or to handle important local problems.

The ruler and guardians can also perform normal work. They can help construct a building, work on a farm, mine resources, negotiate with citizens, train soldiers, or teach apprentices.

Leaving the realm has a cost. While the ruler and guardians are away, they cannot handle government work at home. Local officials must make decisions without them.

Mission outcomes feed back into the realm. A battle may protect a harvest, kill skilled citizens, open a trade route, destroy infrastructure, or create political unrest. The realm provides the party with equipment, supplies, training, recruits, and specialists.

## Citizens and work

Ordinary citizens are autonomous. The player can ask them to do something, create incentives, offer payment, pass laws, or impose obligations. Citizens still decide whether to comply.

Their choices depend on:

- hunger, fatigue, safety, and other needs;
- personal preferences;
- skills and experience;
- dreams and long-term goals;
- short-term plans;
- personality;
- relationships;
- laws and customs;
- payment and expected rewards;
- social duties;
- fear of punishment;
- trust in the ruler;
- available alternatives.

A disciplined character may favor work that supports a distant goal. A hedonistic character may favor immediate comfort. An ambitious and risk-tolerant craftsperson may try to open a business. A cautious person with the same skills may prefer stable employment.

Citizens choose immediate actions through utility calculations. Their dreams, goals, and plans change the value they assign to those actions.

## Projects, tasks, and enterprises

Long-term intentions become projects.

A skilled blacksmith who wants independence might create a project to establish a smithy. The project tracks what is needed:

- permission to use land;
- tools;
- building materials;
- startup funds;
- workers or apprentices;
- access to iron and fuel.

The project then creates useful tasks, such as asking the ruler for support, finding an investor, buying tools, or constructing the workshop.

Once the project succeeds, it becomes an enterprise. The enterprise creates ongoing tasks such as buying materials, producing goods, repairing equipment, selling products, hiring workers, and training apprentices.

The same structure can support farms, shops, mines, schools, military units, temples, public works, and government programs.

## Economy and government

The economic system is part of the simulation.

Different societies may allow:

- government ownership;
- private ownership;
- household ownership;
- guild ownership;
- communal ownership;
- mixed systems;
- temporary use rights without full ownership.

Characters, households, businesses, governments, temples, guilds, and other organizations may all own property.

Ownership is separate from custody and access. A character can own a sword stored at home, carry a government-owned tool, rent a warehouse, or hold the right to farm land owned by someone else.

Storage matters. People who gain wealth need homes, storehouses, banks, or other places to keep goods.

Citizens and organizations exchange goods, labor, rights, and future obligations through negotiated agreements. For example, a farmer may ask the government for land, seed, timber, and money in return for a share of future output.

Agreements are only as strong as the institutions that enforce them. A stable realm may rely on courts and written contracts. A frontier settlement may depend on trust, reputation, and the ruler’s personal power.

## Early progression

The game begins with a fragile settlement.

The first problems are food, shelter, tools, labor, and basic safety. As the settlement becomes secure, the focus moves toward skilled work, businesses, trade, wealth, social conflict, and government.

Later stages introduce multiple settlements, mayors, provinces, governors, armies, diplomacy, war, and the long history of the reincarnating ruler.
